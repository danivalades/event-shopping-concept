The feature, and why it matters

Right now, shopping on ShopMy for an outfit that matches your event’s formality means scrolling lists from the curators you follow, hoping they’ve linked pieces for your occasion. Or you search “wedding guest,” “formal” or “black tie” and scroll lists from curators you don’t know, with no way to filter within a list by color or price.

Most of us know about our events weeks or months out: a sibling’s graduation, a cousin’s wedding, a friend’s engagement party. A wedding alone is a whole weekend, with a welcome party, a reception and a farewell brunch, each needing its own outfit. Nothing in the app knows any of it.

The idea: all your events live in one place, and discovery works for them.

  • Taste firstCurators you already follow and trust come first, named, with things they linked that fit the occasion. Then everyone else’s.
  • Saved onceEach event keeps its date, place and dress code (often on the invite), so recommendations fit both. A garden wedding in summer gets pieces that suit the formality and the setting.
  • Rules of occasions(Next, not built.) Black tie, formal and the other dress codes carry defined parameters, so a garden means no pointy heels that sink into the grass.
  • Filters that stay per eventThe bride wants a white welcome party. Set white once, and that event’s feed stays white.
  • Saves that persist(Next, not built.) A saved piece lives in its event and also shows for other events that fit its formality and climate.
  • Fill the gaps, not the whole lookAlready own the dress for the welcome party? Mark it, and the feed shows only shoes, bags and accessories.

How I'd know if it works

The measure: of shoppers who search for event outfits, the share who buy something on ShopMy within 30 days. Both groups are measured the same way.

The bet: shoppers already have ways to get an outfit: their closet, a friend, a rental, a store they trust. I'm betting that finding one through this feature is easier, because it matches the event's formality, place and weather and puts curators they follow first.

What would prove it wrong: the group that gets the feature buys no more often than the group that doesn't.

How I'd start testing: a randomly chosen half of the shoppers who search "wedding guest dresses," "formal dresses" or "black tie" get the feature. The other half see the app as it is today. No creator has to change anything. Before the buying result means anything, I'd check that most people who start adding an event finish. If they quit partway, the feature never gets a fair try.

About me

I’m Daniela, a product builder in New York.

Most recently, I was a Program Manager at Meta’s Reality Labs, working on the warranty and refurbished-device programs for Ray-Ban Meta glasses and VR headsets. My manager named the outcome and left the how to me, so I got good at spotting where people were working around a problem and building the fix.

I’d love to help build what ShopMy does next, so I made one idea real: a new way to shop for an event, and how we’d know it earns its place.

Replaced a workaround
Requests for refurbished Ray-Ban Meta glasses came in as 10–15 group chats a day. Each one looped in everyone who had passed it along, and all of them were asking what was in stock. I built a live inventory view and a request sheet with one point of contact. Fifteen scheduled minutes a day replaced all of it.
Set prices customers paid
I priced out-of-warranty devices and parts in every program market. The ladder was built so nothing undercut anything else: parts bought one by one never cost less than the refurbished bundle.
Measured what shipped
I defined the programs’ success metrics, and my forecasts decided what went first and where we launched next.
Cut to one idea
For this prototype, I explored about 60 design options and cut four features I’d already built, to keep one idea: show the shopper only what they still need. See every round
